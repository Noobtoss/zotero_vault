What are the most effective ways to evaluate generative AI models for image generation?

Generative AI models are capable of creating realistic and diverse images from scratch or based on some input. However, evaluating the quality and diversity of these images is not a trivial task. How can you measure how well a generative model captures the essence of a given domain, such as faces, landscapes, or artworks? In this article, you will learn about some of the most effective ways to evaluate generative AI models for image generation.

1 Human evaluation

One of the most intuitive and reliable ways to evaluate generative models is to ask human judges to rate the images on various criteria, such as realism, diversity, relevance, and creativity. Human evaluation can capture the subjective aspects of image generation that are hard to quantify by automated metrics. However, human evaluation is also costly, time-consuming, and prone to bias and inconsistency. Therefore, it is often used as a complementary method or a final validation step for generative models.

2 Pixel-based metrics

Another way to evaluate generative models is to compare the generated images with the real images from the same domain using pixel-based metrics, such as mean squared error (MSE), peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), or structural similarity index (SSIM). These metrics measure the pixel-wise similarity or difference between two images, assuming that the closer the pixels are, the better the image quality is. However, pixel-based metrics have some limitations, such as being sensitive to image transformations, ignoring high-level semantic features, and failing to account for diversity and novelty.

3 Feature-based metrics

A more advanced way to evaluate generative models is to use feature-based metrics, which extract high-level features from the images using pre-trained neural networks, such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) or generative adversarial networks (GANs). These features capture the semantic and perceptual aspects of the images, such as shapes, textures, colors, and styles. Feature-based metrics, such as inception score (IS), Fréchet inception distance (FID), or perceptual path length (PPL), compare the feature distributions of the generated and real images, and assess how well the generative model preserves the diversity and quality of the original domain.

4 Task-based metrics

Another way to evaluate generative models is to use task-based metrics, which measure how well the generated images can be used for downstream tasks, such as classification, segmentation, captioning, or retrieval. Task-based metrics can reflect the usefulness and applicability of the generative model for specific purposes and domains. For example, one can use classification accuracy, segmentation accuracy, captioning BLEU score, or retrieval precision and recall as task-based metrics. However, task-based metrics depend on the choice and performance of the downstream models, and may not capture the general aspects of image generation.

5 Novelty-based metrics

A final way to evaluate generative models is to use novelty-based metrics, which measure how novel and diverse the generated images are compared to the existing images in the same or different domains. Novelty-based metrics can reflect the creativity and originality of the generative model, and its ability to explore new regions of the image space. For example, one can use nearest neighbor distance, coverage, or entropy as novelty-based metrics. However, novelty-based metrics may not account for the realism and relevance of the generated images, and may favor unrealistic or irrelevant images.
